Chapter 4: Electricity

Section 1 - Electric Current
Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. When
the ends of a conductor are connected to a battery, the free electrons drift in a
particular direction, and this ordered motion of charge constitutes an electric
current. Current is measured in amperes (A). One ampere equals one coulomb of
charge passing a point in one second. By convention the direction of current is
taken to be the direction of flow of positive charge, which is opposite to the
direction in which electrons actually move.

Section 2 - Potential Difference (Voltage)
To make charge flow through a conductor, work must be done on it. The potential
difference, or voltage, between two points is the work done to move a unit charge
from one point to the other. Voltage is measured in volts (V). A battery
maintains a potential difference across its terminals and therefore acts like a
pump that keeps the charges moving around the circuit. Without a potential
difference there is no sustained current.

Section 3 - Resistance
Resistance is the opposition that a conductor offers to the flow of current. It
arises because the moving electrons collide with the atoms of the conductor and
lose energy. Resistance is measured in ohms. A thin wire has more resistance than
a thick wire of the same material and length, and a long wire has more resistance
than a short one. Resistance also increases with temperature for most metals.

Section 4 - Ohm's Law
Ohm's law states that, at constant temperature, the current through a conductor
is directly proportional to the potential difference across it and inversely
proportional to its resistance. In symbols, V = I x R, where V is the voltage, I
is the current, and R is the resistance. This means that if the voltage across a
fixed resistor is doubled, the current doubles. If instead the voltage is held
constant and the resistance is increased, the current decreases. For example, a
12 V battery connected across a 4 ohm resistor drives a current of 3 A; replacing
it with a 6 ohm resistor at the same 12 V reduces the current to 2 A.

Section 5 - Series and Parallel Circuits
When resistors are connected in series, the same current flows through each and
the total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances. When resistors are
connected in parallel, the voltage across each is the same and the total
resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance. Household wiring uses
parallel connections so that each appliance gets the full supply voltage and can
be switched independently.
